## Confirmed direction

The course teaches analysis and design principles. Students use representations that help them reason and communicate; a particular diagram language is not a learning objective.

Students should be able, without AI, to analyze an unfamiliar (small) problem, propose a design, explain alternatives, and reason about a change using prose, sketches, or pseudocode. AI accelerates work that students understand and can direct.

Students are master's students with a CS major and exposure to major programming paradigms, but limited or no design background. Assume programming fluency; explicitly teach design concepts, distinctions, and reasoning. Domain modeling must not be presented as synonymous with class design. Examples may use objects, functions, data, or state transitions where they make the decision clearer.

The five highest priorities are:

1. Problem framing and requirements.
2. Domain modeling.
3. Contracts and invariants.
4. State and behavior.
5. Responsibility assignment, including cohesion and coupling.

The instructor also considers abstraction, architectural boundaries, design for change and refactoring, design patterns, testing as design feedback, and design tradeoffs important. These should be integrated into the course rather than discarded.

A dedicated class on model validation and analysis will combine practical validation with a small executable model: scenarios, invariants, reachability, deadlocks, and counterexamples. Include one or two slides introducing formal modeling. A dedicated formal-methods tool is not required by this decision; the executable model's language remains open.

The teaching workflow establishes a substantial specification and design before implementation. The course should teach explicit review points for problem scope, assumptions, domain rules, responsibilities, contracts, behavior, and validation evidence before handing work to an implementation agent. Later discoveries can require a reasoned revision of the specification or design. Small executable models and acceptance examples can support these earlier reviews.

The instructor authorized a full assessment redesign and selected the lighter scheme below. Mandatory UML artifacts and named-pattern quotas are removed.

Model execution and prototypes are allowed; a working application is not required for the project. Validation evidence can come from the design and its models. Do not carry forward the previous requirement that every student run an application implementation/TDD loop.

Use a different small domain for each topic. Demonstrations and labs should test transfer of the principles instead of developing one shared system throughout the semester. A scenario can remain coherent within a topic's lecture and exercise; later sessions introduce their own bounded context.

Students may choose their AI tools and models. Require explicit roles, handoffs, and review evidence, including a separate review context. Assessment concerns reasoning and evidence; fixed models, fixed effort levels, paid-provider choices, and exact reproduction of generated wording are not course requirements. Practical setup material must be updated to reflect this decision.

Maintain the administrative/motivational Course 1, expanded technical Course 2, and fully developed Lab 1 packages. Projects use a shared team design. Expect around 100 students across three lab groups, with one instructor supervising all students and a two-hour lab every other week. The instructor chose an individual multiple-choice exam as a scalable assessment, removing graded checkpoints and individual decision notes. Do not require per-student oral defenses. Plan 100 minutes of core lab teaching within the slot.

The source brief also calls for managing the volume of AI-produced work through summaries, detailed explanations, explicit development phases, and separate agents for different phases, especially review. A recurring concern is recognizing when a change to an existing system needs a revised design rather than another local patch.

## Proposed teaching pattern

Each substantive topic starts with a problem and a small student decision. Students make their assumptions and reasoning visible before using AI, then compare alternatives and inspect evidence supporting the selected design.

The semester builds toward a reviewed specification and design before substantial system implementation. Classroom counterexamples and model execution support that preparation. When later implementation uncovers a problem, students identify which earlier decision needs revision and explain its consequences.

An AI-generated proposal can be good, incomplete, or wrong. A successful demonstration must still teach the design principle when the agent gives a good answer. Prepared flawed examples are identified as teaching fixtures; genuine captured runs retain their provenance.

Representations may include a glossary, examples, responsibility tables, operation contracts, state-transition tables, sketches, pseudocode, tests, or diagrams. Students select a representation for a question it helps answer.

Validation and oversight are motivated in Week 1, practiced technically in Week 2, and recur throughout. Later dedicated classes deepen these practices. Summaries should retain links to underlying requirements, decisions, and evidence, so students can inspect consequential details. A separate reviewer receives explicit criteria; students must evaluate its findings.

## Proposed lecture and demonstration structure

Retain 14 teaching weeks. Course 1 is administrative and motivational; its technical content is expanded into Course 2. Previous Courses 2–12 shift to Courses 3–13. The dedicated project-workshop lecture is removed; useful project questions may be discussed in the open Lab 6 according to team needs. Course 14 remains the exam/reflection slot, subject to the announced timetable. Team dossier interviews take place in the final lab.

| Week | Lecture focus | Demonstration or decision exercise |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Administration and motivation | Explain organization, assessment, project responsibilities, public repositories, feedback, and tool choice; discuss why understanding and evidence matter when using AI. |
| 2 | Understanding a problem before delegating its solution | Analyze a small ambiguous brief; identify assumptions; compare a student proposal with an agent proposal; inspect a concrete consequence of one design choice. |
| 3 | Problem framing and requirements | Turn a stakeholder request into scope, goals, measurable quality constraints, open questions, and acceptance examples. Distinguish agreed requirements from suggestions and assumptions. |
| 4 | Domain modeling | Separate concepts, identities, relationships, and rules; introduce simple invariants and challenge a model with examples such as one title and several physical copies. |
| 5 | Responsibility assignment, cohesion, and coupling | Compare two allocations of behavior; follow a change through each design and explain which dependencies it introduces. |
| 6 | Contracts and invariants | State operation preconditions and postconditions, distinguish persistent rules from operation outcomes, and use examples/tests to expose ambiguities. |
| 7 | State and behavior | Develop a lifecycle with legal transitions and guards; investigate an invalid transition and an exceptional path. |
| 8 | Interactions, workflows, and boundaries | Distinguish internal collaboration from external interfaces and the modeled system's boundary. Follow a scenario across collaborating parts; decide who owns coordination and consistency when an operation fails or is repeated. |
| 9 | Model validation and analysis | Use scenario walkthroughs and a small executable model to inspect invariants, reachability, deadlocks, and counterexamples. Revise the model and state the limits of the analysis; introduce formal modeling in one or two slides. |
| 10 | Abstraction and design for change | Introduce a genuine variation; compare a local change, an abstraction, and a relevant pattern, including the cost of each. |
| 11 | Evolving an existing system | Reconstruct the current design from a small repository; compare patching with refactoring; preserve agreed behavior and explain changed assumptions. |
| 12 | Consistency and oversight across delegated work | Follow a requirement through design, implementation, and checks; reconcile a separate reviewer's findings with source evidence and resolve a misleading summary. |
| 13 | Design synthesis and explanation | Analyze an unfamiliar change without AI, explain alternatives, then use AI to investigate one remaining uncertainty. This is formative practice, not a new individual examination. |
| 14 | Multiple-choice exam and course reflection | Assess individual reasoning over supplied scenarios; finalize team dossier scores through clarification interviews in the last lab. Exact exam timetable remains to be arranged. |

The five priorities recur beyond their named weeks. Requirements and domain rules ground every later decision; contracts and behavior supply validation criteria; responsibility assignment recurs when changing boundaries or abstractions.

## Model validation and analysis: proposed class outline

Confirmed scope: practical techniques, a small executable model, and one or two slides about formal modeling.

Proposed learning outcomes:

- Walk a stakeholder scenario through a model and identify a mismatch with the intended problem.
- State a property precisely enough to check it, including relevant assumptions.
- Explore a small model's reachable states and identify an invariant violation or an unexpected inability to progress.
- Read a counterexample as a sequence of events that explains the defect.
- Revise the model and check the repair without claiming more than the analysis establishes.

Proposed demonstration: introduce a new small domain using lifecycle concepts learned in the behavior class. Students predict whether an invariant holds, inspect a failing trace from the executable model, explain the missing condition, and propose a repair. Re-run the exploration and discuss which behaviors were included and excluded. The specific domain and implementation language remain open.

The formal-modeling introduction should connect directly to the exercise:

1. Describe a model with explicit states, transitions, assumptions, and properties. Show how making these precise enables systematic analysis.
2. Explain the scope of a result: checking selected scenarios, exploring all states of a small finite model, and reasoning about more general systems support different claims. A result about the model still needs a justified connection to the intended system and its implementation.

Instructor guidance: distinguish a legitimate terminal state from an unexpected deadlock, and an execution that happens to satisfy a property from analysis covering the model's entire reachable state space. The formal introduction should explain these distinctions through the same example.

## Proposed lab structure

Retain seven 100-minute thematic labs, scheduled according to group timetables. Add optional Lab 0, a 90-minute orientation after Course 1 with a standalone self-study guide for every group. Each lab is scheduled after its two associated courses have been taught: Lab 1 after Courses 1–2, Lab 2 after 3–4, and so on. Do not assume later-course theory from its brief introduction in Course 2. Labs 1–5 each practice an important course topic using independent small scenarios. Only the last two labs are dedicated to projects: Lab 6 is an open session for finishing the project and discussion; Lab 7 is the dossier interview.

Each team creates a public GitHub or GitLab repository when announcing the project on Teams and includes its link. Track progress and identifiable member contributions throughout the semester. AI may assist development, including commits, but the team verifies and takes responsibility for all work. Commit counts are not grading criteria.

| Lab | Courses already taught | Main activity | Evidence produced |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1–2 | Understanding a problem, initial specification, bounded delegation and review. | Scoped brief, questions, scenarios, review decisions, and an explanation of one choice. |
| 2 | 3–4 | Requirements and domain modeling; clarify scope and distinguish identities and relationships. | Requirements, acceptance examples, domain model, assumptions, and review decisions. |
| 3 | 5–6 | Assign responsibilities and specify contracts and invariants. | Responsibility table, operation contracts, before/after examples, and compared alternatives. |
| 4 | 7–8 | State, behavior, and interactions across boundaries. | Legal transitions, interaction traces, rejection/retry examples, and coordination decisions. |
| 5 | 9–10 | Model validation and abstraction under a stated variation. | Properties, check results and limits, compared abstractions, and a justified choice. |
| 6 | 11–12 | Open session for finishing the project, instructor questions, and cross-team discussions. | Progress and revisions relevant to each project, recorded in its public repository; feedback as requested. |
| 7 | 13–14 | Team dossier interview; questions about unclear points. | Final team dossier score on the five published criteria, informed by clarifications. |

## Agreed assessment scheme

| Component | Points | Assessment unit |
|---|---:|---|
| Design dossier | 5 | Once per team |
| Multiple-choice exam | 3 | Individual |
| Attendance | 1 | Individual |
| Automatic point | 1 | Individual |
| Total | 10 | |

The instructor selected a multiple-choice exam to provide individually assessed evidence at a feasible marking cost for roughly 100 students. It replaces graded individual decision notes and the earlier grading scheme. There is no scheduled checkpoint; teams may request feedback at any time during the semester. Classroom exercises remain formative. There is no mandatory oral defense for every student.

The five dossier points cover the five priority areas, one point each. Validation evidence, alternatives, and change reasoning support each relevant criterion. Students choose useful representations. A concise overview connects requirements, design decisions, alternatives, and evidence, with supporting details available for targeted inspection. Do not impose transcript volume, defect-count, UML, or named-pattern quotas.

The exam uses scenarios and small supplied models to assess analysis and design reasoning, including assumptions, responsibilities, invariants, behavior, and validation. Individual construction of a design continues to be practiced in formative exercises; the team dossier assesses constructed design work. The instructor pre-reads each dossier and finalizes its team score in the last lab through questions about unclear points, using the five published criteria. No slide presentation or separate interview score is required. See [assessment-blueprint.md](assessment-blueprint.md) for the proposed exam format and sample items.

Attendance is proportional across both lectures and labs, with equal weight per session: sessions attended divided by the lecture and lab sessions actually held for the student's group. The normal 14+7 schedule gives 21 sessions; cancelled sessions are excluded, and other lab groups are not counted in the denominator.

The resit, which also serves grade improvement (restanță / mărire), is a separate assessment route: a multiple-choice exam worth nine points plus one automatic point. Dossier and attendance marks do not carry over. The regular and resit forms should cover the same core competences with equivalent fresh scenarios; the earlier UML-focused written sample is obsolete for this scheme.

Capacity planning: approximately 33–34 students per lab group, in teams of 3–5, gives roughly 7–11 team interviews. Plan about eight minutes per team, with the remaining time for common instructions, transitions, and feedback. The deliverable (the public repository) must be final about one week before the interview, which allows instructor pre-reading; announce the exact deadline and publish team slots on Teams. Reserve the common final lecture as a possible exam/reflection slot, subject to scheduling. Mark the substantial dossier once per team.
