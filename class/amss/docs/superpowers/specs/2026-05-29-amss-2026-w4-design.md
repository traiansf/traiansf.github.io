## Goal

Author the W4 lecture deck (`class/amss-2026/curs/04-class-diagrams.md`) plus its sibling demo runbook and fallback assets, replacing the current single-stub-slide placeholder. The deck delivers the four content threads the parent spec §2 mandates for W4:

1. Class diagrams as the central structural artifact.
2. Driving AI to produce them.
3. Reading critically — wrong multiplicity, fake associations, missing aggregations.
4. UML literacy targets (the structural reading floor for F1).

These are wrapped in **one architect-critic cycle on a bike-sharing class diagram**, generated live as PlantUML and rendered for critique.

**W4 is two firsts.** It is the **first week with real UML structural notation** — W3 deliberately deferred class diagrams here (W3's verification gate forbade structural UML in its slides). And it is the **lecture half of Lab 2's week** (labs are biweekly: Lab 1 = W2's week, Lab 2 = W4's week), so W4 previews Lab 2 the way W2 previewed Lab 1.

## Decisions (locked during brainstorm)

These apply to W4 specifically; the "course-wide" tag marks decisions inherited from W1/W2/W3.

- **Spine: mirror W1/W2/W3** — welcome → frame → demo → deeper-dive → close. *Course-wide* template.
- **Demo scenario: continue the bike-sharing app.** AI produces a PlantUML class diagram for the domain students already know (Bike, Station, Rental, User, Payment). The full trace becomes visible on one domain: W2 requirements → W3 fare test → W4 structure. Parking lot / ATM / drone stay reserved for Labs 3-4.
- **Notation = core set (literacy floor).** Classes (name / attributes / operations), associations + multiplicity, aggregation vs. composition, generalization / inheritance. Interfaces, abstract classes, and dependency arrows are **name-drops only** — named on one slide as "you will meet these reading AI output," not given teaching slides. This is the F1 structural reading floor: enough to read & critique any AI class diagram and to recognize the parent-spec-named defects.
- **Demo emphasis: architect-critic cycle on structure.** Prompt #1 → AI generates a class diagram → critique targets the parent-spec defects (wrong multiplicity, fake associations, missing aggregation) → prompt #2 re-imposes domain/multiplicity constraints → tighter diagram. Same beat structure as W2's requirements cycle, on a structural artifact.
- **F1 is the anchored literacy thread this week.** W3 anchored F4 (the executable tail of the trace); W4 anchors **F1** — reading & critiquing a UML diagram on the spot is exactly what W4 drills, and exactly what the oral defense tests. Two F1+F3+F4 mentions (frame + close), *course-wide convention* from W2.
- **Reshape, not from-scratch.** Reuse 2025 `class/amss/curs/02-class.md`: the aggregation/composition example, the generalization example, and especially its **over-complicated → simplified diagram pair** (reframed as "AI over-models; here is the simplification critique"). Framing shifts from "here is the notation" to "here is what AI gets wrong about it."
- **Slide density: bullet-light, ≤5 bullets, ≤1 line/bullet.** *Course-wide.*
- **Demo runbook contract: same 10-section structure as W1/W2/W3.** *Course-wide.* Adapted for a demo whose artifact is a *rendered diagram* (PlantUML preview pane in setup; fallback PNGs are diagram renders).
- **Verification: solo dry-run is always sufficient.** *Course-wide.* W4 adds: the AI's generated PlantUML must actually render during dry-run.
- **Glyph safety (W3 lesson).** The beamer/lualatex PDF font drops some unicode (`↔`, `≥`, …). Multiplicities (`1`, `*`, `0..1`) are ASCII and safe; aggregation/composition diamonds and generalization triangles are PlantUML-rendered into the diagram *image*, not font glyphs, so safe. Avoid `↔`/`≥`/etc. in slide bodies. See the project memory note on the glyph constraint.

## 2. Lecture spine, time budget, slide pacing

100-minute slot, ~80 minutes of planned content, ~10-20 minutes of natural slack. Q&A absorbed into segments. Seven segments (slightly leaner than W2/W3's eight: the notation + defect payload is content-dense and needs read time, so total slide count lands ~26 rather than 28).

| # | Segment | Time | Slides | Pacing peg |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Welcome / agenda | 3 min | 1 | Single slide |

| 3 | **Demo: bike-sharing class diagram, 1 cycle** | 15 min | 1 trigger + runbook | Slide stays up during demo |

**Slide-density convention.** Same as W1-W3: bullet-light; overflow goes into `::: notes`.

**Central payload.** Segments 4+5 (~30 min combined) carry the lecture: the notation reading floor plus the AI-defect catalogue. §5.5 is the W4 analogue of W2's failure-modes catalogue and W3's spec-precision catalogue.

## 4. Demo runbook contract

`curs/04-class-diagrams-demo.md` contains the same 10 fixed sections as W1/W2/W3's runbooks — W4-specific content, adapted for a demo whose artifact is a *rendered diagram*.

### 4.1 Setup (pre-class checklist, ~1 min)

Continue.dev open against the canonical course endpoint, agentic chat mode. A **PlantUML preview pane** open in VS Code (the demo's artifact is a diagram — students must *see* it rendered to critique multiplicity and associations; reading raw PlantUML text is not enough). Fallback PNGs queued in a tab. Demo trigger slide on screen.

### 4.2 Architect prompt #1 (verbatim)

> *"Generate a UML class diagram (as PlantUML) for the city bike-sharing app: users rent and return bicycles at stations across a city; payment is by app; staff rebalance bikes between stations."*

Pasted as-is. The diagram is rendered in the preview pane. Reproducibility outweighs naturalness.

### 4.3 Defect catalogue (menu of 6, pick 2-3 live)

Each entry: defect, where to point, critique question. Over-spec'ing the catalogue absorbs model variance. The first three are the parent-spec-named core defects and should anchor the walkthrough.

| # | Defect | Where to point | Critique question |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Wrong multiplicity** | `Rental "*" -- "*" Bike` or `User "1" -- "*" Rental` reversed | *"Read the multiplicity aloud. Can one rental really involve many bikes? What's the real number?"* |
| 2 | **Fake / decorative association** | A line between `User` and `Station` with no label or meaning | *"What does this association mean? Name the verb. If you can't, why is the line there?"* |
| 3 | **Missing aggregation/composition** | `Station -- Bike` as a plain association, not a whole-part | *"A station holds bikes — is that a plain link, or a whole-part? Why isn't it an aggregation?"* |
| 4 | **Invented class** | `DatabaseManager`, `BikeController`, `AppManager` — implementation, not domain | *"Is this a domain concept or an implementation detail? Walk it back to the requirements."* |
| 5 | **God class** | One `BikeShareSystem` class holding most attributes and operations | *"Why does one class do everything? What are the real responsibilities here?"* |
| 6 | **Attribute that should be an association** | `User.rentals: String` / `Station.bikeList: String` | *"Should this attribute be a relationship to another class? What type is it really?"* |

Speaker picks the 2-3 that actually appear. #1 (multiplicity) is near-certain and should anchor.

### 4.4 Critique walkthrough (~5 min)

Speaker walks the chosen 2-3 defects against the rendered diagram. The architect/critic move is named explicitly the first time, framed on structure:

> *"That's the **critic** half — reading the diagram AI drew and asking 'does this structure match the domain?' Next is the **architect** half — I re-prompt with the domain constraints it missed."*

### 4.5 Architect prompt #2 (constructed live, constraint scaffold)

Template provided in the runbook:

> *"Revise the class diagram. A rental is exactly one bike and one user — fix the multiplicities. Make the Station–Bike relationship an aggregation (a station holds bikes). Drop any class that's an implementation detail rather than a domain concept — keep only domain classes."*

The constraint scaffold is the pedagogical pivot: naming the domain rules tightens AI's structure. ~1 min to type, ~2-3 min for AI to revise + render.

### 4.6 Recap (~1 min)

> *"One architect-critic cycle on a class diagram. The first pass drew everything plausible-looking; the critic half read the structure against the domain and found wrong multiplicities and a missing aggregation; the architect half re-prompted with the domain constraints — and the second pass tightened. Reading a diagram against its domain is exactly the F1 skill the oral defense checks, and the loop is what Lab 2 asks you to do this week."*

Points back to the F1 anchor slide.

### 4.7 Make-it-fail reserve

If AI's first diagram is suspiciously clean (low probability — class diagrams reliably over-model), restore the critique surface:

> *"Now expand this into a complete enterprise architecture with all supporting subsystems."*

This near-certainly triggers god classes, invented infrastructure classes (caches, controllers, managers), and over-modeling — restoring the multiplicity / fake-association / invented-class critique surface.

### 4.8 Fallback assets

`04-class-diagrams-demo-fallback/` contains committed PNGs captured during the solo dry-run:

| Filename | Contents |
|---|---|
| `01-fallback-cycle1-diagram.png` | Rendered cycle-1 class diagram with ≥2 visible defects |
| `02-fallback-cycle2-revised.png` | Rendered post-critique revised diagram |
| `03-fallback-make-it-fail.png` | Rendered over-modeled "enterprise architecture" output |

A fourth optional PNG may capture the raw PlantUML text alongside a render. When the procurement decision changes the canonical endpoint, the dry-run reruns and the PNGs refresh.

### 4.9 Time budget reconciliation

Prompt #1 typed (~1 min) + AI generates + render (~2-3 min) + critique walkthrough (~5 min) + prompt #2 typed (~1 min) + AI revises + render (~2-3 min) + recap (~1 min) = ~12-14 min. Variance lives in generation + render latency; the speaker fills it by predicting aloud what the diagram should show.

### 4.10 Lab 2 synergy

The demo is the forerunner of Lab 2's drill. Students replicate the loop on a 1-page spec, iterating ≥2 times and keeping a critique log. The runbook's prompt #2 (domain-constraint scaffold) is the move Lab 2's critique log documents.

## 5. Content contract per segment

For each segment, the **goal** and **required content**. Slide-by-slide bullets are an implementation concern.

### 5.1 Welcome / agenda (3 min)

**Goal:** students know today's spine and how it connects to W3.

**Required content:** one agenda slide + the one-line "W3 pinned behaviour with tests; today we model the structure that realizes it — and Lab 2 this week is your hands-on follow-through."

### 5.2 Frame — from behaviour to structure (8 min)

**Goal:** students see class diagrams as the central structural artifact and recognize F1 as today's anchored skill.

**Required content:**
- "Recap: architect + critic" slide — both roles; cite W3.
- "From behaviour to structure" — W3 pinned *what the system does* (tests); today: *what objects realize it* (classes). Names the demo artifact.
- "Four threads today" — central artifact, driving AI to produce it, reading critically (multiplicity / fake associations / missing aggregation), the literacy floor.
- F1+F3+F4 anchor slide (1st of 2) — anchor F1: today you read & critique an AI-drawn diagram on the spot, which is exactly the oral-defense skill.

### 5.3 Demo trigger (15 min)

**Goal:** students see one architect-critic cycle on a class diagram and the "read the structure against the domain" move live.

**Required content:** single slide with the bike-sharing PlantUML prompt visible, runbook reference, "watch for wrong multiplicity, meaningless associations, and missing whole-part relationships" reminder. Speaker drives the runbook (§4) for ~12-14 min.

### 5.4 Core notation — the reading floor (15 min)

**Goal:** students can read the core class-diagram notation and name each element — the F1 reading floor.

**Required content:**
- "Classes" slide — name / attributes / operations compartments; one rendered PlantUML example (reuse 2025's Book class, reframed).
- "Associations + multiplicity" slide — the most defect-prone element; how to read `1`, `*`, `0..1`, `1..*` aloud; rendered example.
- "Aggregation vs. composition" slide — whole-part; the diamond; shared vs. exclusive lifetime; rendered example (reuse 2025's).
- "Generalization / inheritance" slide — is-a; the triangle; rendered example (reuse 2025's).
- Name-drop slide — interfaces, abstract classes, dependency arrows named in one line each: "you'll meet these reading AI output; today's focus is the four above."
- Optional consolidation slide (a single small bike-sharing diagram using all four) — fits the budget if used.

### 5.5 How AI gets class diagrams wrong (15 min) — central content payload

**Goal:** students can name the AI class-diagram defects and spot them in unseen output (F1 in practice).

**Required content:**
- One slide per core defect (three slides): wrong multiplicity, fake/decorative associations, missing aggregation/composition — each with definition, a rendered or described example, and the critique question that surfaces it.
- Two slides extending to secondary defects: invented classes (implementation leaking into the domain) and god class / over-modeling — using 2025's **over-complicated → simplified diagram pair** as the exhibit (reframed: "AI over-models because it doesn't have to choose what matters; the critic applies the simplification filter").
- One transition slide tying the catalogue to the demo students just watched ("you saw defect #1 — multiplicity — live in cycle 1").
- The attribute-that-should-be-an-association defect appears either as its own item or folded into the fake-association slide, per the speaker's budget.

### 5.6 Reading critically — the F1 drill (12 min)

**Goal:** students internalize a systematic read-order for any AI class diagram and see the critique loop as the structural architect-critic cycle.

**Required content:**
- "How to read an AI class diagram" slide — a fixed read-order: (1) are the classes real domain concepts? (2) are the multiplicities right? (3) does each association name a real relationship? (4) are whole-part relationships captured? This *is* the F1 drill.
- "The critique loop on structure" slide — read → name defects → re-prompt with domain constraints → re-read; the architect-critic loop, same shape as W2/W3 on a structural artifact.
- One slide tying the read-order back to the demo and forward to Lab 2 (you apply this order to your own AI output).
- One slide on the human's judgment: AI can draw plausible structure forever; deciding whether the structure matches the *domain* is the architect-critic call the defense checks.

### 5.7 Bridge to W5 + Lab 2 preview + close (12 min)

**Goal:** students know what Lab 2 asks this week, how W5 extends structural modelling, and where the class sits in the trace.

**Required content:**
- Lab 2 preview slide — drive AI to produce a class diagram from a 1-page spec, iterate ≥2 times, keep a critique log (≤1 page); committed to the lab repo. Vague enough to survive Lab 2's spec still being open.
- W5 teaser — *"one class diagram isn't the whole structure; next week: object, package, component, deployment views, and where AI over- or under-decomposes."*
- F1+F3+F4 anchor slide (2nd of 2) — anchor F1 again (read & critique the diagram cold), with a one-line trace touch: the class is a structural node in the trace (requirement → use case → **class**; the behavioural tail — test — came in W3).
- Closer slide — "next week: other structural views; this week's lab: drive AI to a class diagram and critique it."

### 5.8 Deliberate omissions

Not in W4, by design:

- **Object / package / component / deployment diagrams.** W5 owns these.
- **Behavioural diagrams** (use case, sequence, state, activity). W6/W7.
- **Interfaces / abstract classes / dependency notation as taught content.** Name-drop only (§5.4); they are not part of W4's reading floor.
- **Design patterns.** W8/W9. A class diagram showing a pattern is not a W4 topic.
- **Code generation from diagrams / round-tripping.** Out of scope.
- **Bullet-by-bullet slide authorship.** Implementation, not spec.

## 6. Verification

The lecture is "done" when all of:

1. **Pandoc build succeeds, warning-free.** `make -C class/amss-2026/curs <abs>/04-class-diagrams.html` and `.pdf` both produce well-formed output. **No "Missing character" warnings** in the lualatex run (the W3 glyph lesson — grep the build log).
2. **Class diagrams render and embed.** The rendered PlantUML diagrams (notation slides + over-modeled example) appear as base64 images: `grep -c "data:image" 04-class-diagrams.html` is `>= 4`. A `0` means the diagrams failed to render — blocks done.
3. **Slide count and pacing match the budget.** ~25-32 slides total (target ~26); per-segment subsets within §2 ranges.
4. **F1+F3+F4 mentioned exactly twice** (frame, close), F1 anchored. Drift either direction is a deviation.
5. **Demo runbook is dry-run reproducible.** Instructor runs `04-class-diagrams-demo.md` end-to-end against the chosen endpoint; confirms the AI's generated PlantUML actually renders in the preview pane; captures renders as fallback PNGs; confirms the defect catalogue matches what the model produced. *Blocks delivery.*
6. **Fallback PNGs exist** in `04-class-diagrams-demo-fallback/` per §4.8 (3 required, named as specified).
7. **`static/index.html` links resolve.** `class/amss2026/index.html` → `curs/04-class-diagrams.html` and `.pdf` both load.
8. **Cross-reference linting.** Every reference to another week (W2, W3 recap, W5 teaser, W6 behavioural, W10 traceability) or to Lab 2 names a session that exists in parent-spec §2/§3.
9. **Glyph safety.** No PDF-dropped unicode in slide bodies (`↔`, `≥`, `≤`, …). Multiplicities use ASCII (`1`, `*`, `0..1`); diagram diamonds/triangles are rendered images, not glyphs. Grep slide bodies to confirm.
10. **`*-demo.md` filter still in effect.** Confirm `04-class-diagrams-demo.md` is not picked up by the curs build.
11. **Source-material reuse documented.** Where W4 reuses content from `class/amss/curs/02-class.md` (Book class, aggregation/composition + generalization examples, over-complicated→simplified pair), the deck's `::: notes` cite the 2025 source.
